## Intuition & Overview

You can often guess something from its neighbors. KNN has no training phase: it stores the data, and at prediction time it asks the $k$ closest samples to vote.

## Mathematical Foundation

With $N_k(x)$ the set of the $k$ nearest samples under Euclidean distance, the prediction is:

$$
\hat{y}(x) = \arg\max_{c} \sum_{x_i \in N_k(x)} \mathbb{1}[y_i = c]
$$

$$
d(a, b) = \sqrt{\sum_{j} (a_j - b_j)^2}
$$

At $k = 1$, the decision regions form the Voronoi diagram of the samples. Larger $k$ averages over more points, trading variance for bias.

## Step-by-Step Breakdown

1. Store the <Term id="training-set">training set</Term>.
2. For a query, compute its distance to every sample.
3. Take the $k$ closest.
4. Return the majority class. This implementation breaks ties in favor of the class of the nearest neighbor.

## Interactive Sandbox

Each step raises $k$ by two. <Term id="accuracy">Accuracy</Term> is measured leave-one-out, because training accuracy at $k = 1$ is always 100% and tells you nothing.

## Strengths, Limitations & Real-world Trade-offs

**Strengths**

- No training cost, and new data can be added instantly.
- Boundaries can take any shape.

**Limitations**

- Naive prediction costs $O(nd)$ per query, and all the data must stay in memory.
- It is sensitive to <Term id="feature">feature</Term> scale, like [any geometric distance](/distance-metrics/overview/#scale-your-features-first).
- In high dimensions distances concentrate, so "nearest" loses meaning.
