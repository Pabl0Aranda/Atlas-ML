## Intuition & Overview

One [decision tree](/algorithms/decision-tree/) is unstable: change a few samples and you can get a very different tree. A random forest turns that weakness into a strength. It grows many trees that each see a slightly different version of the problem, then lets them vote. Their individual mistakes differ, so most of them cancel out.

## Mathematical Foundation

Each tree $b$ trains on a bootstrap sample: $n$ draws from the data, with replacement. The forest predicts the class with the most votes:

$$
\hat{y}(x) = \arg\max_{c} \sum_{b=1}^{B} \mathbb{1}[\,T_b(x) = c\,]
$$

Averaging only helps when the trees disagree. For $B$ trees with variance $\sigma^2$ and pairwise correlation $\rho$, the variance of their average is:

$$
\rho\,\sigma^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{B}\,\sigma^2
$$

More trees shrink the second term, but only lower correlation shrinks the first. That is why each split considers a random subset of the <Term id="feature">features</Term>.

A bootstrap sample leaves out each point with probability $(1 - 1/n)^n \approx 0.37$. Those out-of-bag points act as a free <Term id="validation-set">validation set</Term> for that tree.

## Step-by-Step Breakdown

1. Draw a bootstrap sample of the <Term id="training-set">training data</Term>.
2. Grow a tree on it, choosing from a random subset of features at each split.
3. Score the tree on the samples it never saw, and add those votes to the out-of-bag tally.
4. Repeat until the forest has enough trees.
5. Predict by majority vote.

## Strengths, Limitations & Real-world Trade-offs

**Strengths**

- A strong default on tabular data, with little tuning.
- Adding trees does not cause <Term id="overfitting">overfitting</Term>. Past a point it only costs time.
- Out-of-bag scoring gives a validation estimate without a separate split.

**Limitations**

- Hundreds of trees are slower and larger than one, and far harder to read.
- Boundaries are still built from axis-aligned splits.
- Tuned [gradient boosting](/algorithms/gradient-boosting/) often scores higher on the same data.
