## Intuition & Overview

Drop $k$ flags on a map and let every point walk to its nearest flag. Then move each flag to the middle of the crowd around it. Repeat until nobody switches flags. Those flags are the centroids, and the crowds are the clusters.

## Mathematical Foundation

k-means looks for a partition $C_1, \dots, C_k$ that minimizes the within-cluster sum of squares, also called inertia:

$$
J = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \sum_{x_i \in C_j} \lVert x_i - \mu_j \rVert^2
$$

For fixed assignments, the best centroid is the cluster mean:

$$
\mu_j = \frac{1}{|C_j|} \sum_{x_i \in C_j} x_i
$$

Neither step can increase $J$, and there are finitely many partitions, so the algorithm always terminates. It reaches a local minimum, which is not necessarily the global one.

## Step-by-Step Breakdown

1. **Seed** the centroids with k-means++: pick one sample, then sample each next centroid with probability proportional to its squared distance from the nearest existing centroid.
2. **Assign** each point to its nearest centroid.
3. **Update** each centroid to the mean of its assigned points.
4. **Repeat** steps 2 and 3 until no assignment changes.

## Strengths, Limitations & Real-world Trade-offs

**Strengths**

- Simple and fast: each iteration costs $O(nkd)$.
- Scales to large data, especially in its mini-batch variant.

**Limitations**

- You must choose $k$ in advance.
- It assumes compact, similarly sized clusters.
- Results depend on initialization, so production code runs several restarts.
- <Term id="feature">Features</Term> need comparable scales, because distance mixes them directly.

For curved or density-based structure, consider DBSCAN or hierarchical clustering, both listed in the [catalog](/catalog/).
