## Intuition & Overview

A straight line cannot follow a curve, but a sum of powers of $x$ can. Polynomial regression is still linear in its weights, so the same training procedure as [linear regression](/algorithms/linear-regression/) applies. The degree controls flexibility, and with it the risk of [overfitting](/concepts/bias-variance/).

## Mathematical Foundation

The model expands the input into powers:

$$
\hat{y} = \sum_{j=0}^{d} w_j x^j
$$

With an L2 penalty $\lambda$ on every weight but the bias, the objective becomes:

$$
L(w) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\hat{y}_i - y_i)^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{d} w_j^2
$$

and each gradient component is:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_j} = \frac{2}{n} \sum_{i} (\hat{y}_i - y_i)\, x_i^{\,j} + 2 \lambda w_j
$$

## Step-by-Step Breakdown

1. Choose a degree $d$ and map each $x$ to $(1, x, x^2, \dots, x^d)$.
2. Start with all weights at zero.
3. Apply gradient descent on the regularized loss.
4. Compare degrees on held-out data, because training loss only improves as $d$ grows.

## Strengths, Limitations & Real-world Trade-offs

**Strengths**

- Handles smooth curvature with a small, simple model.
- Inherits the convex loss of linear regression, so there are no bad local minima.

**Limitations**

- High degrees oscillate, especially near the edges of the data.
- Extrapolation is unreliable because polynomials grow without bound.
- Feature count grows quickly with several inputs, and high powers are poorly conditioned. Scale the inputs first.
