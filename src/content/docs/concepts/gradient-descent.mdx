Gradient descent minimizes a loss by repeatedly stepping downhill:

$$
w \leftarrow w - \alpha \nabla L(w)
$$

The gradient $\nabla L(w)$ points in the direction of steepest increase, so we move against it. The learning rate $\alpha$ sets the step size.

## Choosing the learning rate

- **Too small:** progress is slow.
- **Well chosen:** the loss falls steadily.
- **Too large:** updates overshoot, and the loss can oscillate or diverge.

## Momentum

Plain descent forgets every step as soon as it takes it. Momentum keeps a share $\beta$ of the last step $v$ and adds the new one to it:

$$
v \leftarrow \beta v - \alpha \nabla L(w)
$$

$$
w \leftarrow w + v
$$

Steps that keep pointing the same way build up speed, and steps that flip back and forth cancel out. That helps most in long, narrow valleys, where plain descent bounces across the valley and makes little progress along it. With $\beta = 0$ this is plain gradient descent again.

## Variants

| Variant | Gradient computed on | Trade-off |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Batch | The full dataset | Stable, but costly per step |
| Stochastic | One sample | Cheap and noisy |
| Mini-batch | A small subset | The usual choice in deep learning |

Adaptive methods such as Adam adjust the step size for each parameter. Every sandbox on this site that uses gradients runs full-batch descent, so each step is deterministic.
