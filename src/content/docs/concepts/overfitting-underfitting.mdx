The goal of machine learning isn't to do well on the data you already have. It's to do well on data you haven't seen yet, which is called **generalization**. Overfitting and underfitting are the two main ways a model fails at that.

Think of a student preparing for an exam:

- **Underfitting** is the student who barely studied. They do badly on the practice questions and on the exam.
- **Overfitting** is the student who memorized the practice answers word for word. They ace the practice questions, then fail when the exam asks something new.
- **A good fit** is the student who learned the ideas. They do well on both.

## The Two Problems

| | Underfitting | Good fit | Overfitting |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **What happens** | The model is too simple to capture the pattern | The model captures the pattern and ignores the noise | The model also learns the noise |
| **Training score** | Low | High | Very high |
| **Validation score** | Low | High | Much lower than training |
| **Gap between them** | Small | Small | Large |
| **Also known as** | [High bias](/concepts/bias-variance/) | Balanced | [High variance](/concepts/bias-variance/) |

Every dataset has two parts: the real **pattern**, and random **noise** that won't repeat in new data. A model can't tell them apart on its own. Too little flexibility misses the pattern, and too much flexibility memorizes the noise too.

## See It Happen

This is [K-nearest neighbors](/algorithms/knn/) on noisy data, with a quarter of the samples held out. Small $k$ makes the model very flexible, because each prediction depends on only a few samples. Large $k$ makes it rigid, because each prediction averages over almost everything. Move the slider from left to right.

<FitExplorer client:visible />

Averaged over 20 random samples, the three regimes look like this:

| $k$ | Training accuracy | Test accuracy | Gap | Diagnosis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 100.0% | 81.9% | 18.1 points | Overfitting |
| 25 | 90.3% | 88.2% | 2.1 points | Good fit |
| 85 | 71.4% | 68.6% | 2.7 points | Underfitting |

Notice that the model with the best training score has the worst gap. At $k = 1$, every sample is its own nearest neighbor, so a training score of 100% is guaranteed. It says nothing about how good the model is.

As the model gets more rigid, the training score falls, while the test score rises and later falls. **The best model sits near that peak, not where the training score is highest.**

Here the peak is wide: test accuracy stays near 88% from $k = 15$ to $k = 75$, so many settings work about equally well. It only collapses at the far end, when $k$ gets close to the 90 training samples and every prediction turns into a vote of almost the whole dataset. Real problems often have a wide good region like this, so there's no need to hunt for one perfect value.

Overfitting also depends on the data. On the clean moons dataset, $k = 1$ reaches 100% on test data too, because there's almost no noise to memorize.

## How to Diagnose It

Compare training and validation scores, always on data the model didn't train on:

1. **Is the training score poor?** The model is underfitting, so it can't even explain the data it has seen.
2. **Is the training score good, but validation much worse?** The model is overfitting.
3. **Are both good and close together?** The fit is healthy, so confirm it once on a [test set](/testing/overview/).

What counts as "much worse" depends on the problem. Compare the gap with the spread across [cross-validation](/validation/cross-validation/) folds, since small samples produce noisy scores. The explorer above calls a gap above 8 points overfitting, and a test score 5 points below the best underfitting. Those are rules of thumb for this example, not universal limits.

[Learning curves](/validation/overview/#learning-curves) help too, because they show what more data would do:

- **Underfitting:** both curves level off low and close together, so more data won't help.
- **Overfitting:** a gap remains, and it narrows as data grows, so more data should help.

## How to Fix It

The two problems need opposite treatments, so diagnose first.

| To fix underfitting | To fix overfitting |
| --- | --- |
| Use a more flexible model | Use a simpler model |
| Add better features | Remove noisy or irrelevant features |
| Reduce regularization | Add [regularization](/algorithms/ridge-regression/) |
| Train for longer | Stop early, when validation stops improving |
| | Collect more training data |
| | Combine models, as in a [random forest](/algorithms/random-forest/) |
| | Use data augmentation or dropout in neural networks |

More data helps with overfitting, but it can't fix underfitting. A straight line stays a straight line however many samples you give it.

## The Control That Sets the Balance

Most algorithms have a setting that moves them between the two extremes:

| Algorithm | More flexible, risks overfitting | More rigid, risks underfitting |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [KNN](/algorithms/knn/) | Small $k$ | Large $k$ |
| [Polynomial regression](/algorithms/polynomial-regression/) | High degree | Low degree |
| [Decision tree](/algorithms/decision-tree/) | Deep tree | Shallow tree |
| [SVM](/algorithms/svm/) | Large $C$, large $\gamma$ | Small $C$, small $\gamma$ |
| [Ridge](/algorithms/ridge-regression/) and [Lasso](/algorithms/lasso-regression/) | Small penalty $\lambda$ | Large penalty $\lambda$ |
| [Neural network](/algorithms/mlp/) | More layers and units | Fewer layers and units |

Choosing this setting is what [hyperparameter tuning](/validation/hyperparameter-tuning/) is for. Always choose it from validation scores, because the training score will always favor the most flexible option.

## Common Misunderstandings

- **"100% training accuracy means a great model."** It often means the opposite, so never judge a model by its training score.
- **"Overfitting only affects complex models."** A simple model can overfit too when there are very few samples.
- **"More data always helps."** It helps with overfitting, but it does little for an underfitting model.
- **"A little gap means something is wrong."** A small gap is normal, since a model will nearly always do slightly better on data it has seen.
- **"Overfitting only happens during training."** You can also overfit to a validation set by trying many models against it. That's why the final [test set](/testing/overview/) is used only once.
- **"A suspiciously good score is good news."** A validation score that seems too good often comes from [data leakage](/data-preparation/overview/#data-leakage), not from a great model.
