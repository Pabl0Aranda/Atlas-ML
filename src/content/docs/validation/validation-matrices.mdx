A single [indicator](/validation/indicators/) compresses a model into one number. A matrix keeps the detail: it shows where a model succeeds, which mistakes it makes, and how stable its results are.

| Matrix | Rows and columns | The question it answers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Binary confusion matrix](#binary-confusion-matrix) | Actual and predicted class | What kinds of mistakes does the model make? |
| [Multi-class confusion matrix](#multi-class-confusion-matrix) | Actual and predicted class | Which classes get mixed up? |
| [Normalized confusion matrix](#normalized-confusion-matrix) | Actual and predicted class, as rates | How good is the model per class, whatever its size? |
| [Cost matrix](#cost-matrix) | Actual and predicted class, as prices | What do these mistakes actually cost? |
| [Fold score matrix](#fold-score-matrix) | Models and folds | Is one model really better, and is it stable? |
| [Hyperparameter grid](#hyperparameter-grid) | Two settings | Which settings work, and how sensitive are they? |
| [Paired comparison table](#paired-comparison-table) | Model A and model B outcomes | Is the difference between two models real? |

## Binary Confusion Matrix

The foundation of all the others. It counts every prediction by what was predicted and what was true.

| | Predicted positive | Predicted negative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Actually positive** | True positive (TP) | False negative (FN) |
| **Actually negative** | False positive (FP) | True negative (TN) |

**Uses:** any two-class problem, such as fraud, disease or spam. It is the source of <Term id="precision">precision</Term>, recall, F1 and most other [indicators](/validation/indicators/).

**How to read it:**

1. Start with the diagonal, TP and TN. Those are the correct predictions.
2. Then read the other two cells separately, because they are different mistakes. FN are the misses, and FP are the false alarms.
3. Read along the top row for <Term id="recall">recall</Term>: how many real positives were caught?
4. Read down the left column for precision: how many alarms were real?
5. Compare row totals to see how imbalanced the classes are.

**Why it matters:** two models with the same <Term id="accuracy">accuracy</Term> can make completely different mistakes, and only the matrix shows that. It also makes a useless model easy to spot, since a model that always predicts one class leaves one column empty.

:::caution[Check the layout first]
Layouts differ between tools. scikit-learn puts actual classes on rows and sorts labels, so for labels 0 and 1 it prints `[[TN, FP], [FN, TP]]`, with TP at the bottom right. That is the opposite corner from the table above. Other tools swap rows and columns. Always check before reading a matrix.
:::

## Multi-class Confusion Matrix

The same idea with one row and one column per class. This example has 150 images:

| Actual ↓ / Predicted → | Cat | Dog | Bird | Recall |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Cat** | **40** | 8 | 2 | 80.0% |
| **Dog** | 5 | **42** | 3 | 84.0% |
| **Bird** | 1 | 4 | **45** | 90.0% |
| **Precision** | 87.0% | 77.8% | 90.0% | Accuracy 84.7% |

**Uses:** any problem with three or more classes, such as image labels, document topics or customer segments.

**How to read it:**

1. The diagonal holds the correct predictions, so a strong model has a bright diagonal.
2. Each row shows where one true class went. Of 50 cats, 8 were called dogs.
3. Each column shows what a prediction really contained. Of 54 "dog" predictions, 12 were wrong.
4. Look for the largest number off the diagonal. Here it is cats predicted as dogs.
5. Compare the two sides of the diagonal. There are 8 cats called dogs but only 5 dogs called cats, so the confusion leans one way.

**Why it matters:** it tells you what to fix. Two classes that are often confused may need more examples, better <Term id="feature">features</Term>, or merging. It often reveals labeling problems too.

## Normalized Confusion Matrix

Raw counts are hard to compare when classes differ in size. Dividing each cell by a total turns counts into rates. The choice of total changes the meaning, as this screening example with 10 sick people and 990 healthy people shows:

| Dividing by | Top-left cell | Bottom-left cell | Each cell then means |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nothing (counts) | 8 | 40 | Number of samples |
| Row total | 80.0% | 4.0% | Of the real cases, the share given this prediction |
| Column total | 16.7% | 83.3% | Of these predictions, the share that were this class |
| Grand total | 0.8% | 4.0% | Share of all samples |

**Uses:** imbalanced data, comparing datasets of different sizes, and heatmaps where a large class would otherwise wash out the colors.

**How to read it:**

- **By row:** the diagonal shows recall per class. This is the most common choice.
- **By column:** the diagonal shows precision per class.
- **Overall:** the diagonal adds up to accuracy.

**Why it matters:** the same 40 false positives look like a minor 4.0% of healthy people, or a major 83.3% of all alarms. Both are true, and the right view depends on your question. Keep the raw counts in view too, because 100% of 2 samples proves very little.

## Cost Matrix

A cost matrix is not a result. It's the price list you decide on, which you combine with a confusion matrix.

| | Predicted positive | Predicted negative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Actually positive** | 0 | 50 (a miss) |
| **Actually negative** | 1 (a false alarm) | 0 |

$$
\text{Total cost} = \sum_{i,j} \text{count}_{ij} \cdot \text{cost}_{ij}
$$

Here are two models on the same 1,000 people, with those prices:

| Model | TP | FN | FP | TN | Accuracy | Total cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Screening | 8 | 2 | 40 | 950 | 95.8% | **140** |
| Cautious | 5 | 5 | 5 | 985 | **99.0%** | 255 |

**Uses:** medicine, fraud, credit and any case where errors have different prices. It's also the principled way to choose a decision threshold.

**How to read it:** multiply each cell of the confusion matrix by its price and add them up. Lower is better. The ratio of the prices matters more than their exact values.

**Why it matters:** it brings the real-world goal into the evaluation. Above, the model with the better accuracy is the more expensive one. Setting the prices also forces an honest conversation with whoever owns the decision.

## Fold Score Matrix

When you use [cross-validation](/validation/cross-validation/), each model gets one score per fold. Keep the whole table instead of only the mean. These are real results from this site's KNN on the noisy dataset:

| Model | Fold 1 | Fold 2 | Fold 3 | Fold 4 | Fold 5 | Mean |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $k = 1$ | 83.3% | 75.0% | 91.7% | 83.3% | 91.7% | 85.0% |
| $k = 5$ | 87.5% | 87.5% | 95.8% | 95.8% | 100.0% | 93.3% |
| $k = 25$ | 87.5% | 87.5% | 95.8% | 95.8% | 100.0% | 93.3% |

**Uses:** comparing candidate models and judging how stable each one is.

**How to read it:**

1. Read along a row to see stability. $k = 1$ moves between 75.0% and 91.7%, so its mean alone would hide a lot.
2. Read down a column to compare models on identical data. $k = 5$ beats $k = 1$ in every fold, which is stronger evidence than the means alone. It ties $k = 25$ in every fold, so nothing here separates those two.
3. Look for a column that is low for every model. Folds 1 and 2 are harder here, which may point to unusual samples.

**Why it matters:** a mean can come from steady results or from wild swings. With only 24 samples per fold, a single sample moves a score by about 4 points, so small gaps between means are often noise. Models must share the same folds for this comparison to be fair.

## Hyperparameter Grid

When tuning two settings, each cell holds a validation score. These are real results from this site's RBF [SVM](/algorithms/svm/) on moons, using 30 <Term id="held-out-data">held-out</Term> samples:

| Penalty $C$ ↓ / Width $\gamma$ → | 0.5 | 5 | 30 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **0.1** | 83.3% | 90.0% | 100.0% |
| **1** | 83.3% | 100.0% | 100.0% |
| **20** | 96.7% | 100.0% | 100.0% |

**Uses:** [grid search](/validation/hyperparameter-tuning/), usually drawn as a heatmap.

**How to read it:**

1. Look for a broad region of good scores, not a single best cell. Here it covers the bottom-right corner.
2. Prefer the middle of that region, because settings on a plateau are more likely to hold up on new data.
3. If the best cell sits on the edge of the grid, widen the search in that direction.
4. Be wary of one good cell surrounded by poor ones. It's probably luck.

**Why it matters:** it shows how sensitive the model is, which a single "best parameters" result hides. Picking the best of many cells also flatters the score, so confirm the choice on a separate [test set](/testing/overview/). With only 30 samples, this table can't separate the 100% cells from each other.

## Paired Comparison Table

To compare two models on the same samples, count where they agree and where they differ:

| | Model B correct | Model B wrong |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Model A correct** | 180 | 12 |
| **Model A wrong** | 4 | 4 |

**Uses:** deciding whether a new model truly beats the current one, with McNemar's test.

**How to read it:** ignore the cells where the models agree, since they say nothing about which is better. Only the 12 and the 4 matter, where $b$ and $c$ are those two counts:

$$
\chi^2 = \frac{(\lvert b - c \rvert - 1)^2}{b + c} = \frac{(12 - 4 - 1)^2}{16} = 3.06
$$

A value above 3.84 is significant at the 5% level. At 3.06, the data doesn't show a real difference, even though model A scored 96.0% against 92.0%.

**Why it matters:** it stops you from replacing a model because of noise. It's also more sensitive than comparing two accuracy figures, because both models see identical samples.

## Choosing a Matrix

| Your question | Use |
| --- | --- |
| What mistakes does the model make? | Confusion matrix |
| Which classes get mixed up? | Multi-class confusion matrix |
| Are classes very different in size? | Normalized confusion matrix |
| Do errors have different prices? | Cost matrix |
| Which model is better and more stable? | Fold score matrix |
| Which settings should I use? | Hyperparameter grid |
| Is the improvement real? | Paired comparison table |
